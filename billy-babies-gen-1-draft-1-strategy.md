# Billy Babies — Gen 1, Draft 1 Design Strategy

This is a proposed decision guide for designing the first 50 playable cards from the 199-Billy Gen 1 roster. Its targets are starting hypotheses to test, not established balance findings or changes to the agreed game rules.

References: [game rules](billy-babies-rules.md), [potential effects](billy-babies-effects.md), and [Gen 1 roster](billy-babies-gen-1.md).

Current draft artifacts: [50-slot card sheet and first 12 card drafts](billy-babies-gen-1-draft-1-cards.md), with a [proposed timing supplement](billy-babies-gen-1-draft-1-timing.md). The other 38 cards remain design briefs; full deck construction and match playtests are still ahead.

## What this draft should establish

The first 50 cards should support several recognizable ways to play, meaningful card choices within those strategies, and useful combinations between strategies. They should also reveal whether the game's scoring, quality endings, cancellation, and exhaustion work well together.

Success means players can build different legal decks, explain why they chose their cards, and make different decisions about plays, discards, wand use, and round endings. A large number of unique effect descriptions is not sufficient evidence.

Each player builds a 20-card deck from the same 50-card pool. Apply the existing singleton rule, maximum 5 Rare cards and 2 Mythic cards, and unchanged deck composition between rounds. The first 50 are a playable subset of Gen 1, not a replacement roster.

## Concentrate on three primary strategies

For this first pool, favor three supported strategies over distributing cards across every potential strategy in the effects document. Dice, wand, quality, and exhaustion effects can support these decks without each needing a complete independent deck yet.

| Primary strategy | Intended play pattern | Starting effect candidates | Intended weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Emotion shifting | Build useful emotion leads, then move surplus points into lanes that can change the checkmark result. | Joy to spare, Feelings in motion, Best friends, redistribution, and narrow-deficit bonuses. | Needs points to move; an early ending or pressure on its source lanes can disrupt the plan. |
| Discard and recovery | Choose plays and normal discards as a pair, earn discard rewards, and recover timely options. | Parting harmony, Welcome back, Familiar face, A little of everything, and bounded discard exchanges. | Small-hand constraints and setup costs; recovery alone does not score enough to win. |
| Table building and sacrifice | Establish useful persistent cards, then decide whether to preserve their future value or cash them in. | A proper send-off, Parting gift, Pass it on, Family gathering, protection, and targeted removal. | Setup takes turns; sacrifice loses table magic and reverses quality contributions, while opposing removal can interrupt the engine. |

Table building and sacrifice should allow different choices within the third strategy. A player who protects an engine and a player who regularly sacrifices cards need not choose identical lists.

Emotion labels in existing examples are provisional. Distribute useful combinations across all six emotions rather than letting the current concentration of Love and Peaceful examples dictate the whole set.

## Allocate the 50 design slots

Count each card once by its primary design purpose. Secondary strategy tags can overlap freely.

| Primary allocation | Cards | Purpose |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Emotion shifting | 12 | Establish, redistribute, and defend points. |
| Discard and recovery | 12 | Make discard choices productive and recover useful cards. |
| Table building and sacrifice | 12 | Supply table cards, sacrifice actions, rewards, and protection. |
| Shared options | 14 | Fill scoring gaps, answer threats, and support hybrid decks. |
| **Total** | **50** | |

Within each 12-card strategy allocation, start with roughly 4 cards that enable the plan, 3 that reward it, 3 flexible supporting cards, and 2 answers or protection cards. A card may serve several roles; assign it one primary role for counting. Adjust this mix when actual deck construction reveals a shortage.

For the 14 shared slots, start with 6 flexible scoring cards, 3 removal or disruption cards, 1 protection card, 2 hand-selection cards, and 2 quality-management cards. These categories describe their main purpose, not a requirement for identical mechanics.

Aim for at least 22 credible card candidates for each primary strategy, counting relevant shared cards and connections to the other strategies. Check that the pool permits multiple legal 20-card selections after rarity caps. Do not count a card as support just because any deck is allowed to include it.

### Select from the approved roster

Use a starting rarity target of **16 Common, 19 Uncommon, 11 Rare, and 4 Mythic cards**. This keeps the sample close to Gen 1's existing distribution while totaling 50. Adjust the selection if necessary for a coherent test pool; preserve each selected Billy's approved rarity, name, appearance, and generation.

Rarity currently reflects visual concept. Do not automatically make higher-rarity cards stronger, more complex, or indispensable to a strategy. Each strategy needs sufficient Common and Uncommon support to fill the minimum 13 such cards in a legal deck.

Choose a Billy whose character fits a needed gameplay role. For example, a comforting creature could soften a bad roll, and a generous creature could reward sacrifice. Appearance is a source of flavor, not a reason to add an unsupported mechanic.

## Decisions to make for every card

1. **Name its job.** State what the card contributes to a deck and when the player would prefer it over another candidate.
2. **Identify its connections.** For a synergy card, name at least three other proposed cards or a sufficiently common card trait that supports it. Avoid rewards that have only one possible enabler in the 50-card pool.
3. **Identify the tradeoff.** Use weaker immediate scoring, lower magic, a conditional reward, a real resource cost, setup time, or vulnerability where appropriate. Not every card needs an explicit penalty sentence.
4. **Describe the play-or-discard choice.** Explain when the card should be kept, played, or intentionally discarded. A narrow card may sometimes be the correct discard without being a failed design.
5. **Check how it helps win.** Connect its benefit to emotion checkmarks, quality position, ending the round, or the table-magic tiebreaker. Dominating one emotion alone is not a complete scoring plan.
6. **Confirm resolution.** Specify targets, timing, persistence, costs, limits, and quality reversal. The designer should be able to resolve the card without inventing a rule during play.

Shared cards can have a clear standalone job without requiring synergy. They still need reasons for one deck to prefer them over another option.

## Keep complexity controlled

Prefer one main text effect per card. Reuse familiar mechanics with different emotion profiles, conditions, and costs where that creates a different deck role.

For Draft 1, focus on immediate gains and transfers, bounded recurring triggers, normal-discard rewards, hand/discard exchanges, targeted removal, sacrifice, and straightforward protection. Include only a small number of start-of-turn engines so their value can be tested.

Defer temporary silence, opponent choices during resolution, counter-based countdowns, and extensive round-history tracking unless a prototype deck has a specific need for one. They remain in the effects pool for later use. Avoid adding extra turns or unrestricted extra plays to this draft.

Use multiple distinct cards to support the same plan, but check the combined reward when several persistent cards observe one event. A once-per-turn limit on each card does not limit the total reward from the whole table.

## Establish a provisional stat baseline

Before assigning all 50 cards numerical values, write down the candidate shared quality pool, the permitted quality assignments per card, and starting ranges for quality points and base magic. The current rules do not establish those card-design ranges. Keep these decisions in the draft's design notes and version changes so comparisons remain interpretable.

Use a few simple scoring cards as reference points before pricing conditional effects. The existing emotion dice have these average results per die:

| Strength | Average points |
| --- | ---: |
| Normal | 2.50 |
| Strong | 3.67 |
| Brutal | 4.83 |

These averages are only a starting reference. Points in a close lane can matter more than a much larger gain in a lane already won. A transfer can affect two lanes; stealing points changes both players' scores in one lane.

Compare complete cards across these dimensions:

- Immediate emotion scoring and its distribution across lanes.
- Quality movement, including whether it can end resolution before text or wand gain.
- Wand gain and how close it brings the player to the 200-magic cancellation threshold.
- Table magic and the value of persistent effects over a short versus a long round.
- Setup requirements, available partners, vulnerability, and usefulness when drawn late.
- Whether staying on the table removes the card from later reshuffles, changing both available draws and exhaustion timing.

A mandatory reroll chosen before seeing a die's result is not a guaranteed scoring improvement. Its value may come from reroll rewards or changing uncertainty. Price it accordingly.

Use baseline magic for initial tests when no fatigue or underuse adjustments apply under the rules. Before expanding toward 199, test whether threshold-based cards behave sensibly across the permitted adjusted-magic range as well.

## Resolve only the rule details this draft needs

Write a small, versioned prototype timing supplement before playing cards that depend on it. Keep it distinct from the agreed rules until adopted. It should settle:

- When successful-play watchers, table-entry watchers, and other pending rewards resolve, including their order when several trigger together.
- What qualifies as sacrifice, how self-departure rewards are recorded, and how quality reversal can stop remaining effects.
- How start-of-turn abilities are ordered.
- Whether quality movement caused by text keeps a card on the table, and which card owns movement that later reverses.
- Whether hand/discard exchanges qualify as recovery without becoming draw attempts, and how invalid targets are handled.

Retain the existing cancellation window and replacement-play procedure. A canceled card supplies no effects. Reaching an endpoint ends resolution immediately, and the exhaustion ending still requires both players to reach at least two exhaustions.

Quality is worth two checkmarks and is not an automatic win at an endpoint. Every prototype deck should have a way to contest enough emotion lanes even when its preferred qualities are absent. Use the same declared shared quality pool for all matchups; do not tailor it to the decks being compared.

## Build the draft in this order

1. **Create the 50-slot card sheet.** Assign primary strategy, role, secondary connections, and provisional complexity before writing every effect.
2. **Design 12 representative cards.** Start with four per primary strategy covering an enabler, a reward, a flexible card, and an answer. Pair them with provisional stats and actual Gen 1 Billies. Walk through their interactions to expose timing gaps.
3. **Complete the other 24 strategy slots.** Add alternative enablers and rewards that make the strategies work without one exact card combination.
4. **Fill the 14 shared slots.** Use these to address observed scoring, interaction, hand-selection, and quality-management gaps.
5. **Build five legal 20-card decks.** Create one for each primary strategy, a shifting/discard hybrid, and a discard/sacrifice hybrid. Record plausible alternative cards and why each was cut.
6. **Resolve rules questions, then play full matches.** Iterate on the smallest set of cards that explains a problem. Keep each tested card version and its deck lists together.

For each card, record: Billy name and existing rarity; primary and secondary strategies; role; dice by emotion and strength; qualities and points; base magic; exact text; persistence and timing; intended partners; tradeoff; and revision notes. The effects document's names and SYN identifiers can be referenced as sources without treating their current wording or numbers as final.

## What to learn from playtests

Begin with the three primary decks to find obvious rules or support problems. Once they function, test all ten pairings among the five decks, initially twice per pairing with the starting player swapped. Record qualities, draws, deck versions, and player assignments. Twenty matches are an exploratory pass, not proof of balanced win rates.

Play complete best-of-three matches so carried wand charge and changing qualities are represented. Include ordinary shuffled games as well as separate controlled checks for cancellation of a key card, quality-ending removal, a missing synergy piece, and empty-deck penalties. Keep controlled scenario results separate from match outcomes.

| Question | Useful evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Do the strategies feel different? | Different normal discards, wand decisions, setup priorities, and preferred round lengths. |
| Can a deck function without its favorite card? | Useful plays and plausible scoring plans after that card is discarded, canceled, or never drawn. |
| Are there real deck-building choices? | Multiple defensible inclusions and cuts within rarity limits; hybrids sacrifice something meaningful for their added flexibility. |
| Are engines answerable? | Opponents can disrupt, outscore, or end the round against them without needing one exact answer. |
| Does each card justify its place? | Situations where it is deliberately played or used for a discard interaction, rather than being consistently unwanted. |
| Are rewards accumulating too quickly? | Number and total value of triggers caused by one action, along with how long the sources survive. |
| Are round endings satisfying? | Players understand why the round ended and which checkmarks or tiebreaker decided it. |
| Is any shared card crowding out choices? | Repeated inclusion across every strategy without a meaningful deck-specific reason. |

After each session, capture the most interesting decision, the most confusing interaction, and the least useful card. Review those alongside match outcomes. Avoid balancing solely around total points or a small sample of wins.

## When Draft 1 is ready to expand

Move beyond the first 50 when:

- Three primary decks and at least two hybrids are legal, functional, and make noticeably different decisions.
- Each primary strategy has alternative card choices and survives the loss of a key card.
- All six emotions have useful support somewhere in the pool, and decks can adapt to changing qualities without changing their card lists.
- No included effect has an unresolved timing interpretation, repeatable loop without a stopping condition, or way to bypass the agreed round-ending rules.
- Persistent engines, recovery, removal, and wand generation have been evaluated in combination, with no obvious unanswered dominant package remaining in the tested decks.
- Remaining weaknesses are recorded as specific card or support gaps rather than answered by adding unrelated new mechanics.

Use the next cards to address those gaps, add alternative versions of successful roles, and introduce another strategy only when enough supporting slots can be committed to it.
